Design the First Two Minutes

Stop asking whether you feel ready. Specify what starting looks like.

Win for this lesson: Before today ends, you will create a start plan for one important action that is specific enough to do without negotiating with yourself.

The problem is usually not the whole task

“Work on the project” is not an action. It has no clear first movement, so your brain can keep it safely in the future. A start is physical and observable: open a particular file, write one heading, send one message, put shoes on, or place the document on the desk.

We are not trying to manufacture motivation. We are building a cue-driven response. A review of habit formation describes habits as automatic responses triggered by contexts repeatedly associated with behaviour. That is why a stable cue and a tiny repeatable action beat grand promises.

When [specific cue] happens, I will do [physical first action] for two minutes.

Source: Gardner, Lally & Wardle (2012), “Making health habitual”.

Make the action smaller than your resistance

Bad: “After breakfast, I will work on my presentation.”
Better: “After I put my cup in the sink, I will open the presentation and write the title of the next slide for two minutes.”
Bad: “I will get fit.”
Better: “After I change out of work clothes, I will put on my walking shoes and walk to the gate for two minutes.”

Two minutes is not a trick that guarantees a long session. Sometimes you stop after two minutes. That still counts: you kept the promise to start. Reliability is the base layer; duration comes later.

Build today’s start plan

Quick check

Which plan is designed to produce an actual start?

Today’s field test

  1. Use the planner above for one important action.
  2. Do it at the cue today. Start the timer for two minutes.
  3. Record one line somewhere you will see tomorrow: date — action — cue — started: yes/no.

If you miss it, do not write “I failed.” Write what actually happened before the cue and what made the first action hard. That is system data.
